1.2 Types of variables

We use the Madrid listings introduced in the first part of the topic. The file has 31 columns (described in data/processed/idealista/README.md); in this course we mostly use the following fourteen:

listings = pl.read_csv("data/processed/idealista/madrid_listings_2018.csv").select(
    "listing_id", "district", "zone", "condition",
    "price", "price_m2", "area_m2", "bedrooms", "bathrooms",
    "floor", "lift", "terrace", "construction_year", "dist_centre_km",
)
  • listing_id: identifier of the listing on the portal.
  • district: district of Madrid where the dwelling is (the city has 21).
  • zone: Centre (the seven central districts, roughly inside the M-30 ring road) or Periphery (the other fourteen).
  • condition: state of the dwelling: To renovate, Good (second-hand in good condition) or New (newly built).
  • price: asking price, in euros.
  • price_m2: asking price per square metre of floor area, in euros.
  • area_m2: floor area of the dwelling, in m².
  • bedrooms and bathrooms: number of bedrooms (0 for a studio) and of bathrooms.
  • floor: floor of the dwelling (−1 basement, 0 ground floor, 1 first floor…). Missing for a few listings.
  • lift and terrace: true if the building has a lift and if the dwelling has a terrace.
  • construction_year: year in which the building was built.
  • dist_centre_km: distance to the city centre (Puerta del Sol), in km.

Variables and data

NoteDefinition
  • A variable is a characteristic of the individuals that can take different values, or categories, from one individual to another.
  • Data are the observed values of one or more variables on a set of individuals.
  • We write \(x_i\) for the value of the variable \(X\) for individual \(i\), \(i = 1, \dots, n\).
  • With \(p\) variables \(X_1, \dots, X_p\), the data form the data matrix \(\mathbf{X} = (x_{ij})\) of size \(n \times p\), where \(x_{ij}\) is the value of variable \(X_j\) for individual \(i\).

In the data matrix each row is an individual and each column a variable. This is exactly the structure of a Polars DataFrame, with \(n = 75{,}735\) rows and \(p = 14\) columns here:

Classification of variables

The type of a variable decides which tables, graphs and summaries make sense.

Qualitative (categorical) variables: their values are qualities, not quantities.

  • Nominal: the categories have no natural order. For example, the district or the zone.
  • Ordinal: the categories have a natural order, but the distance between them is not defined. For example, the condition of the dwelling: “to renovate” is worse than “good”, which is worse than “new”, but it makes no sense to ask how much worse.
  • Binary (dichotomous) variables have only two categories, such as having a lift or not. They are usually coded as 1 (yes) and 0 (no), and then their mean is the proportion of ones.

Quantitative variables: their values are numbers that express an amount, measured in some units.

  • Discrete: they take separate values, usually whole numbers, that can be counted. For example, the number of bedrooms.
  • Continuous: they can take, at least in theory, any value in an interval. For example, the price, the floor area or the distance to the centre.
WarningBeing recorded as a number does not make a variable quantitative

Many qualitative variables are recorded with numbers: postcodes, district codes (01 for Centro, 04 for Salamanca…) or identifiers. Computing the mean of these codes makes no sense. The type of a variable depends on what it measures, not on how it is stored.

TipDiscrete or continuous in practice

Every measurement has limited precision: prices are rounded to the euro and areas to the square metre. Strictly, all observed data are discrete. In practice we treat a variable as continuous when it takes many different values and intermediate values make sense. The price is continuous for practical purposes; the number of bedrooms is not.

Measurement scales

A finer classification, due to Stevens (1946), looks at the operations that make sense with the values of a variable:

The third column gives a precise criterion. A scale is defined by the transformations that do not change the information it carries: the condition of a dwelling could be coded 1, 2, 3 or 0, 5, 100, since any increasing coding keeps the order; temperatures can be expressed in °C or °F, \(F = 32 + 1.8\,C\); prices in euros or in dollars. A statement about the data is meaningful for a scale if its truth does not depend on which admissible transformation is used. For example, “the median condition in the centre is higher than in the periphery” is meaningful for ordinal data, because increasing transformations preserve medians, but a comparison of mean codes is not (Topic 2).

The difference between the last two scales is the meaning of zero. On a ratio scale zero means absence of the quantity (0 m², 0 €), so it makes sense to say that a flat of 120 m² is twice as large as one of 60 m². On an interval scale zero is a convention. A building from 2000 is not “twice as old” as one from 1000, and the ground floor is numbered 0 only by custom (in other countries it is the first floor). Differences, however, do make sense: a building from 1990 is 30 years newer than one from 1960. Most quantitative variables in economics are on a ratio scale, which allows percentages of change and relative measures such as the coefficient of variation (Topic 2).

NoteHistorical note: S. S. Stevens and the scales of measurement

The psychologist Stanley Smith Stevens proposed the four scales in a short paper in Science in 1946 (Stevens 1946), after a long debate on whether sensations such as loudness could be “measured” at all. His classification is still the standard one, although it has been criticised as too rigid: for example, answers on a scale from 1 to 5 are ordinal, but they are often averaged as if they were interval data.

Types of data by structure

How the data are organised in time also matters:

  • Cross-sectional data: many individuals observed at the same time. For example, the dwellings for sale in Madrid in 2018, our data.
  • Time series: a single individual or aggregate observed at many moments. For example, the INE housing price index, one value per quarter.
  • Panel (longitudinal) data: many individuals, the same ones, observed at several moments. For example, the asking price of the same dwellings in each quarter they stay on the market: we can see whether the seller lowers the price.

Two remarks:

  • construction_year is stored as an integer, and its mean can be computed. It makes sense (the “average age” of the buildings), because differences between years are meaningful.
  • condition is stored as text (String), so Polars does not know that its categories are ordered. If we sort them, the order is alphabetical, which is not the logical one:

To declare an ordinal variable we use the type pl.Enum, which keeps the categories in the order given. Sorting and comparisons then follow the logical order:

condition_order = pl.Enum(["To renovate", "Good", "New"])
condition = listings["condition"].cast(condition_order)

condition.unique().sort()

# Proportion of listings in good condition or better
round((condition >= "Good").mean(), 3)

The last expression uses two ideas of this topic: the comparison \(\geq\) only makes sense because the variable is ordinal, and the mean of a binary variable (true = 1, false = 0) is a proportion.

Summary of the topic

  • Statistics studies how to draw conclusions from data that show variability, measuring the uncertainty of those conclusions.
  • Descriptive statistics summarises the observed data. Inference generalises from the sample to the population, using probability.
  • The population (\(N\)) is the set of interest; the sample (\(n\)) is the part observed. A representative sample must be chosen at random, and a larger sample does not correct a selection bias.
  • A parameter describes the population, and is fixed and unknown. A statistic is computed from a sample and changes from sample to sample.
  • Variables are qualitative (nominal or ordinal) or quantitative (discrete or continuous). The measurement scale decides which operations and summaries make sense.

Exercises

Exercise 1 (Classifying variables) Classify the following variables of a dwelling by type (qualitative nominal or ordinal, quantitative discrete or continuous) and measurement scale:

  1. Postcode.
  2. Energy rating (A, B, C, …, G).
  3. Number of bedrooms.
  4. Monthly rent (€).
  5. Type of heating (none, individual, central).
  6. Distance to the nearest metro station (km).
  7. Year of the last renovation.
  8. Owner’s satisfaction with the neighbourhood, on a scale from 1 to 5.
TipSolution

  1. Qualitative nominal, although it is written with numbers.

  2. Qualitative ordinal: A is better than B, but the “distance” between ratings is not defined.

  3. Quantitative discrete, ratio scale (a flat with 4 bedrooms has twice as many as one with 2).

  4. Quantitative continuous, ratio scale.

  5. Qualitative nominal.

  6. Quantitative continuous, ratio scale.

  7. Quantitative discrete, interval scale: subtracting years makes sense (years since the renovation), dividing them does not.

  8. Qualitative ordinal. Computing its mean is common, but it assumes that the distance between 1 and 2 is the same as between 4 and 5.

Exercise 2 (Types in Python)  

  1. Compute, with Polars, the proportion of listings with a terrace.
  2. Polars computes listings["listing_id"] without complaining if we sort it. Would it make sense to compute its median? Why?
  3. Declare zone as an Enum. Does the order of its categories matter?
TipSolution 
print(f"a) Proportion with terrace: {listings['terrace'].mean():.3f}")
zone = listings["zone"].cast(pl.Enum(["Centre", "Periphery"]))
print("c)", zone.unique().sort().to_list())

c) ['Centre', 'Periphery']

  1. About a third of the listings have a terrace. The mean of a binary variable is a proportion.

  2. No. The identifier only labels each listing; it is a qualitative variable, even if it can be sorted as text.

  3. The order of an Enum only matters for ordinal variables. zone is nominal, so any order is valid; we only choose one for tables and graphs.
